## Quick answer

**A hot upstairs is an attic insulation problem, not an air conditioning problem, in the large majority of two story homes.** On a 100 degree Sacramento Valley afternoon, an under insulated attic can reach 150 degrees, and that heat radiates straight down through the ceiling into your bedrooms. Adding insulation to the correct R-value and sealing the air leaks underneath it is what closes the gap for good.

> **Quick verdict:** If your upstairs runs more than 3 degrees hotter than your downstairs on a hot afternoon, check your attic before you touch your AC. As of August 2026, a typical valley attic upgrade runs $1,500 to $4,500 and takes one day.

## Why the second floor always loses

Three things stack against your upstairs every summer, and they compound.

**Heat rises.** The warm air in your house naturally migrates up. That is basic physics and no amount of equipment changes it.

**Your attic is an oven.** Direct sun on the roof can push attic temperatures to 150 degrees on a day when it is 100 outside. That superheated air sits directly above your bedroom ceilings.

**Insulation is the only thing in between.** If that layer is thin, settled, or full of gaps, the attic heat conducts down into the living space all afternoon and keeps radiating well past sunset. It is why upstairs bedrooms often feel worst around 9pm, hours after the sun went down.

Homes in [Yuba City](/service-areas/yuba-city), Marysville, and across Sutter County feel this harder than most. We get long stretches above 95 degrees, and a lot of the housing stock was built before current energy codes existed.

## How much of a temperature gap is normal?

A well insulated two story home should hold within 2 to 3 degrees between floors. That is the benchmark.

| Temperature gap | What it usually means | What it usually costs to fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 to 3 degrees | Normal. Insulation and air sealing are doing their job. | Nothing |
| 4 to 7 degrees | Borderline. Often thin or settled attic insulation. | $1,500 to $2,500 top up |
| 8 to 15 degrees | A real insulation or air sealing problem. | $2,000 to $4,500 |
| 15+ degrees | Usually missing insulation, major air leaks, or duct problems. | $3,000 to $7,000+ |

Measure it yourself on a hot afternoon. Put a thermometer upstairs and another downstairs, same distance from a vent, and check them both around 4pm. That number tells you more than any guess.

## The five causes we actually find

After years in attics across the valley and foothills, the same handful of causes come up again and again.

**1. Not enough insulation.** According to ENERGY STAR, attics in most of the country should be insulated to between R-38 and R-60, and California's Title 24 energy code sets roughly R-38 as the attic baseline for our climate zones. That is around 12 to 14 inches of blown-in material. A lot of older valley homes have 3 to 6 inches, which is somewhere near R-13. If you can see the tops of your ceiling joists when you look across the attic floor, you do not have enough.

**2. Settled or compressed insulation.** Insulation loses R-value as it packs down over the years. Twenty year old blown-in fiberglass that started at R-38 may be performing closer to R-22 today. It looks like insulation. It stopped working like insulation.

**3. Air leaks under the insulation.** This is the one homeowners almost never suspect. According to the U.S. Department of Energy, air leaks account for roughly 25 to 30 percent of the energy used to heat and cool a typical home. Gaps around recessed lights, the top plates of walls, plumbing penetrations, and the attic hatch let conditioned air escape upward all day. Insulation slows heat transfer, but it does not stop air movement. Laying more insulation over unsealed gaps is like putting a sweater over an open window.

**4. Pest damage or moisture.** Rodents tunnel through insulation and flatten it. Roof leaks soak it. Wet or contaminated material has to come out before anything new goes in, which is what [insulation removal](/services/insulation-removal) exists for.

**5. Duct losses in the attic.** If your ducts run through a 150 degree attic and they are leaky or poorly insulated, you are cooling the attic before the air ever reaches your bedroom.

For a fuller checklist of warning signs, we wrote about [the signs your home needs new insulation](/blog/signs-your-home-needs-new-insulation).

## Why adding a bigger AC rarely fixes it

Homeowners often reach for a bigger air conditioner first. It is the most expensive way to treat the symptom.

An oversized unit cools the air fast, shuts off, and never runs long enough to pull humidity out or to even out the temperature between floors. You get short blasts of cold followed by the upstairs climbing right back. Meanwhile the attic keeps pushing heat down and your equipment keeps cycling.

Insulation and air sealing reduce how much heat gets in at all. That is a smaller job than replacing an HVAC system and it makes whatever equipment you already own work better.

## What actually fixes it

We run every attic through the same three steps. We call it **the Clean, Sealed, Covered method**, and skipping any one of them is why so many insulation jobs underperform.

1. **Clean.** If the existing material is wet, settled, or pest damaged, it comes out. Installing fresh insulation on top of failing material buries the problem.
2. **Sealed.** Before anything new goes down, the air leaks get sealed. Top plates, can lights, plumbing and wiring penetrations, and the attic hatch. This step is skipped constantly by low bidders and it is the single biggest driver of whether the job actually works.
3. **Covered.** Fresh insulation to the right R-value. [Blown-in](/services/blown-in-insulation) is the usual choice for attic floors because it fills irregular spaces and covers joists evenly. [Spray foam](/services/spray-foam-insulation) is the stronger option when you want air sealing and insulation in one step, particularly if you are conditioning the attic space itself.

That middle step is where the money is. According to the U.S. Department of Energy, sealing air leaks and adding insulation together save a typical homeowner around 15 percent on heating and cooling costs, and neither one delivers that on its own.

## Is late summer a bad time to do this?

It is actually one of the better times, for two reasons.

You can feel the problem right now, which means you can verify the fix immediately. Take your upstairs and downstairs readings before the work and again a week after.

And doing it now means the same insulation is in place for winter. Insulation is not a summer product. The layer that keeps 150 degree attic heat out of your bedrooms in August is the same layer that keeps your furnace heat inside in January. Most homeowners see it show up on both the summer and winter bills.

The one honest caveat: attic work in peak heat is genuinely hard on crews, so we start early in the morning during heat waves. That is a scheduling detail, not a reason to wait.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much hotter should my upstairs be than my downstairs?**
No more than 2 to 3 degrees in a well insulated two story home. A gap of 8 degrees or more on a hot afternoon usually indicates an attic insulation or air sealing problem rather than an equipment problem. Measure both floors around 4pm on a hot day to get an honest number.

**Will adding attic insulation actually cool my upstairs?**
Yes, when the attic is the cause, and it usually is. Bringing an attic from roughly R-13 up to R-38 with proper air sealing typically narrows the gap between floors to within a few degrees and shortens how long your air conditioner runs each afternoon. It does not replace a failing AC unit, but it stops the heat load that overwhelms a working one.

**Do I need to remove my old insulation first?**
Only if it is wet, settled, contaminated, or pest damaged. Clean, dry insulation that is simply too thin can often be topped up with blown-in material. An installer should look before answering, because removal adds roughly $1,000 to $2,500 to a job and nobody should quote it sight unseen.

**How long does attic insulation work take?**
Most single family attics are a one day job. Removal of old material adds time, typically pushing it to one and a half or two days. You can stay in the house while the work happens.

**Is spray foam or blown-in better for a hot upstairs?**
Blown-in is the more common and more affordable answer for an attic floor, and it solves most temperature gaps when paired with air sealing. Spray foam makes more sense when you want insulation and air sealing in a single step, or when ducts and equipment live in the attic and you want that space brought inside the conditioned envelope.
